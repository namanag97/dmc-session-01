Before anything · 5 minutes

Ask what the number is for.

Your CEO isn't collecting statistics. He's about to decide something, and this number will tip it one way or the other. Read his note again and find the decision hiding in it.

Then agree, out loud, on one sentence: "He will use this to decide whether to…". Everything else follows from that sentence. If the table can't agree, the facilitator is playing the CEO today. Go and ask him.

Then · 10 minutes

Follow one story from start to finish.

Every number is made of thousands of small stories: an order placed, shipped, delivered, sometimes sent back. Before you count them, understand one of them completely.

Ask your analyst to walk you through a single real case, with every date, and then to list all the different ways such a case can end. Sketch it on paper: the start on the left, the endings on the right. Ask yourselves which endings belong in the CEO's number, which don't, and which aren't finished yet.

If you'd like a starting point, say something like this 
Take the question "". Pick one real case from the data that this is about and walk me through it from beginning to end, with every date and every change of status, in plain English. Then list every way such a case can end, and how many August cases ended each way.
Then · 10 minutes

Find the forks.

A fork is anywhere two sensible people would count differently. Does a cancelled order count? Is a customer "new" if we've seen their phone number before? Does August mean when it was ordered, or when it arrived?

There are usually five to eight forks in a question like yours, and each one moves the number. Ask the analyst to list them without choosing, and to point out anything odd in the data while it's there. Then choose at every fork yourselves, using the sentence from the first step. Write the choices down.

If you'd like a starting point, say something like this 
Before calculating anything for "": list every choice I'd have to make to turn this into one number, with the options for each, and roughly how much the number would move. Also tell me anything odd you notice in the data: duplicates, test records, missing dates, things still in progress. Don't pick, and don't give me the answer yet.
Then · 10 minutes

Write the definition in seven lines.

This is the most valuable thing you'll make today. It's what makes your number yours, rather than the analyst's. Plain words, one line each.

  • Whythe decision it serves
  • Who or whatwhich customers, orders or products count
  • What happenedthe event that counts: placed, delivered, paid, returned…
  • Whenwhich date puts it in August
  • Ruleswhat's in, what's out, how the odd cases are treated
  • Checkwhat you'll compare it against
  • Claimwhat the number proves, and what it doesn't
The revenue example from the talk, written this way
  • Whythe top line the board sees for August
  • Who or whatreal customer orders, webstore and marketplaces; test orders out
  • What happenedthe parcel was delivered
  • Whendelivered date in August, whatever the order date
  • Rulesprice without GST; minus returns that came back in August; cancelled, undelivered and in-transit orders out
  • Checkagainst the finance ledger and the money actually collected
  • Claimwhat customers paid us for goods they kept; not cash in the bank, not profit

Then · 10 minutes

Check it a second way.

A number you've reached once is a guess. Ask the analyst to get to it again by a different route, through a different part of the data, and to explain every bit of the gap. Then ask what could make your number wrong, and how much each thing could move it.

If the gap is big and nobody can explain it, you've missed a fork. Go back for that one fork. Don't start over.

If you'd like a starting point, say something like this 
Check that two ways. First, calculate the same thing again by a different route through the data; show both numbers and explain every bit of the difference. Second, tell me what in the data could make my number wrong, and how much each thing could move it. Finish with the number you'd stand behind and one sentence on what it doesn't prove.
Last · 5 minutes

Decide what you'll say.

You'll have three minutes in front of the room. Say these, in this order. The last one is the one everybody forgets.

  • The questionas the CEO asked it
  • The decisionit serves
  • The forksthe two or three that mattered most
  • The definitionyou chose, and why
  • The numberand what was counted
  • The checkand how big the gap was
  • The conclusionone sentence he can act on
  • The limitswhat the number does not prove
One last thing

Defend the number, not the tool. "The AI said so" isn't an answer. Say what it counts, what it leaves out, and why you chose that.

One number with its limits beats three without.
